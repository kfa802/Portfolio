01

Code and craft

Code is where I get to build things myself. Most of my projects are made in Unity with C#, where I write the systems that make a game work: how the story unlocks step by step, how the player moves and interacts, and how all the parts talk to each other.

What I enjoy most is finding the structure behind a project: working out which systems it needs, how they depend on each other, and then building them so they work together as one whole. Mapping it out first, like in the Plan Bee diagram, keeps the code clean and logical, even when a project grows.

And I rarely stay in just one place. One day I'm writing systems, the next I'm working on interaction, visuals or storytelling, and I enjoy all of it.

02

How it looks matters too

I care as much about how a project looks and communicates as how it works. For me, creative expression happens through technology just as much as through visuals.

I make the graphic design, illustrations and small animations that give an experience its personality: steam rising from a pot, an icon that tells you to shake your phone, objects you can pick up in a game. And I edit video to tell a project's story.

3D work

From model to shader

I model in Blender and like the technical side of visuals too. I modelled this little gnome, then used it to recreate the Dead Eye effect from Red Dead Redemption 2 as a shader: the world fades to a warm sepia, the target lights up in red and gets marked with X's, before everything snaps back to full colour.

Atmosphere & environments

Setting the mood

A world feels real when the light, weather and small details are right. In the Women's Suffrage game I worked on the environment settings and added rain and other particle effects to make the streets feel alive.

I like building that kind of mood in other projects too, from the candlelit study in the Harry Potter escape room to the dark, rainy night in Resident Evil (but worse).

Photoshop

Playing with light and colour

I also edit and stylize in-game shots in Photoshop, playing with light, colour and mood.

Video editing

Telling the story

Editing is where a project's story comes together. Every semester project ends with a short film that presents it, and I enjoy shaping those: choosing shots, finding the pacing and cutting to music.

I edit in DaVinci Resolve and use After Effects for animation and motion. Outside school I also cut my own edits to music, like In Dreams, which I made in After Effects.

03

Problem-Based Learning

At AAU, every semester is a group project built around a real problem. We go round the loop until the design fits the people it's for:

  1. Define
  2. Research
  3. Prototype
  4. Test
  5. Refine

It means starting with a problem, not a solution. We research it, involve the people it affects and let what we learn shape what we build. Every project ends in a report and a group exam, so I'm used to backing up design choices with research and data.

Working in groups has shaped me just as much. I've learned to communicate clearly, give and take honest feedback and trust each other's strengths, because the best ideas rarely come from one person. It has also taught me to plan together, share the workload and keep a common goal in sight when deadlines get tight.

04

Real partners, real audiences

Many of my projects are made together with partners outside the university, like Den Blå Planet and Arbejdermuseet. Designing for real people with real goals keeps the work grounded.

Partners shape the work in ways a classroom can't. At Den Blå Planet, we tested our sea turtle game with 41 fourth-graders visiting the aquarium, and most said they had learned more about the turtles' challenges. At Arbejdermuseet, the museum's historical expertise helped us tell the story accurately, in a way 7th–9th graders could understand.

Seeing people play something I helped make, and actually take something away from it, is what makes this work meaningful to me.
